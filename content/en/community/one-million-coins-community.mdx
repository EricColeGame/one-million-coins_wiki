Scooping coins by hand sounds like a joke until you remember there are exactly one million of them waiting in the vault. **One Million Coins**, the short first-person incremental game from developer ShoXx, turns that absurd premise into a focused grind of upgrades, hauling, and bunker restoration. Because it launched so recently, the One Million Coins community is still small — and that makes early player experience unusually valuable for anyone planning a clean vault clear.

If you are looking for a One Million Coins community hub, the honest answer is that one is being built right now, mostly in review sections, store forums, and achievement discussions. This guide gathers what is actually known, separates confirmed mechanics from guesswork, and gives you a practical path through the vault.

## What One Million Coins Actually Is

One Million Coins is a first-person incremental game where the entire map is a single underground vault filled with exactly 1,000,000 coins. You begin with empty pockets and bare hands, scoop gold directly off the pile, carry it to a deposit station, and spend the proceeds on better equipment. Developer ShoXx — who also handles publishing — describes the game as a "short, focused" experience that most players should be able to work through in roughly two hours.

That brevity is the point. This is not an idle game you leave running overnight. It is a hands-on resource management sprint with a defined finish line, and every upgrade visibly changes how fast you move, how much you carry, and how quickly the pile shrinks.

## The Core Loop: Bank Every Haul

The rhythm of One Million Coins is simple to describe and surprisingly satisfying to execute. Every trip through the vault follows the same four beats, and mastering them is what separates a slow crawl from a gold-collecting machine.

| Stage | What You Do | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Collect | Scoop coins by hand, then with progressively stronger tools | Tool strength determines how much you pull per action |
| Carry | Haul the load to a deposit station | Capacity limits how much of that haul survives the trip |
| Bank | Convert collected gold into spendable funds | Banked funds are the only currency for upgrades |
| Upgrade | Buy tools, bags, and skill nodes | Each upgrade accelerates the next collection cycle |

Early on, the bottleneck is your hands. Mid-game, it is capacity — you can gather faster than you can carry. Late game, it is deposit throughput, which is why restoring bunker infrastructure matters so much. Recognizing which stage is currently limiting you is the single most useful habit you can build.

Community reports from the first days after launch consistently describe the same turning point: the moment a capacity upgrade lands and your run stops feeling like chores and starts feeling like a production line.

## Skill Tree Priorities: Where to Spend First

The headline progression system is a **250+ node skill tree** that the developer describes as spanning seven upgrade paths. The store page names movement, reach, collection speed, tool strength, capacity, and deposit rate among the improvements available. With that many nodes, spreading your points evenly is the fastest way to stall out.

| Upgrade Category | Best Early? | What It Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| Movement | Yes | Long walks between the pile and the deposit station |
| Capacity | Yes | Constant return trips with a nearly empty pouch |
| Collection speed | Situational | Slow scooping, once capacity is comfortable |
| Tool strength | Mid-game | How much each swing or scoop actually yields |
| Reach | Mid-game | Coins that sit just outside your current range |
| Deposit rate | Late game | Queueing at the station after big hauls |
| Infrastructure restoration | Throughout | Cargo lifts, conveyors, and deeper vault access |

A practical rule: if you find yourself bored, you probably need movement or capacity. If you find yourself frustrated, you probably need tool strength or reach. Boredom and frustration are useful signals in an incremental game, because they point directly at whichever stat is lagging behind the rest of your build.

Because the One Million Coins community is still young, no single "solved" build has emerged yet. That is genuinely good news for new players — experimentation is cheap, respecs are rarely punished in a two-hour game, and the tree is wide enough that two players can finish with completely different setups.

## Tools, Bags, and Bunker Infrastructure

Progression in One Million Coins is not only about numbers on a skill tree. You also physically rebuild the vault around you. The developer lists deposit stations, cargo lifts, conveyors, and other bunker infrastructure as restoration targets, and each one changes how the space functions rather than simply making a number bigger.

| Unlock Type | Function | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Pouches and backpacks | Increase carry capacity | High — early bottleneck |
| Stronger collection tools | Increase coins gathered per action | High — mid-game |
| Deposit station upgrades | Speed up converting gold to funds | Medium |
| Cargo lifts | Move large volumes vertically through the bunker | Medium to high |
| Conveyors | Automate part of the transport chain | High for late-game pacing |
| Deeper vault access | Opens new excavation areas | Do it when progress slows |

The through-line is automation. One Million Coins is tagged as an automation and resource management game for a reason: the goal is to reach a point where huge waves of coins arrive without you personally hauling every last one. If a purchase does not either speed up collection or remove a manual step, it is probably a detour.

## Practical Tips From Early Player Experience

These recommendations blend confirmed mechanics with patterns that show up repeatedly in early player experience. Treat them as starting points, not commandments.

- **Bank often at first.** Small, frequent deposits fund your first capacity upgrade faster than one giant haul.
- **Fix the walk before the scoop.** Movement upgrades reduce total trip time, which compounds across every future haul.
- **Buy capacity in bursts.** Two capacity upgrades back to back often beat spreading the same funds across three different stats.
- **Restore infrastructure before pushing deeper.** New areas are only worth opening when your existing loop is efficient.
- **Watch for the two-hour arc.** Knowing the game is short encourages aggressive spending rather than hoarding funds.
- **Save anytime.** The game supports saving at any point and is explicitly designed to be playable at your own pace, so sessions do not need to be long.
- **Check the leaderboards after your first clear.** They are far more interesting once you know the route.

## FAQ

**Is there an official One Million Coins community hub or Discord?**
No public community hub had been established at the time of writing. Discussion is currently concentrated in Steam reviews and store forums, which means the One Million Coins community is effectively self-organizing around Valve's own tools rather than a dedicated server.

**How long does One Million Coins take to finish?**
The developer states that most players should be able to work through the vault in around two hours. Speed-focused players chasing leaderboard positions will likely finish faster, while completionists hunting all 16 achievements may spend longer.

**Is One Million Coins multiplayer or co-op?**
No. It is a single-player experience, though Steam Cloud, Family Sharing, and leaderboards give it a light social layer. That combination is why the One Million Coins community tends to compare results rather than play together.

**Do I need to enjoy incremental games to like this?**
Not necessarily. Because it is first-person and hands-on, One Million Coins plays more like a short resource management sprint than a menu-driven idle game. If you like watching a system get visibly faster over a couple of hours, the structure will land even if the genre normally does not.

**Is the game still being updated?**
Nothing in the available material confirms a post-launch roadmap. With 16 achievements, a finite vault, and a two-hour design target, the game appears intentionally complete at release — a rarity in a genre built on endless numbers.
